“Our Armenian culture finds these European ideas unacceptable”: Language and belonging in Armenian queer communities

Lima Boudakian, Valijarvi, and Kahn

Abstract:

In this paper we examine the linguistic practices of the queer community in Armenia. We rely on the theoretical notions of communities of practice, language ideologies and attitudes, and (dis)belonging to analyse the language use reported in a dataset of anonymous survey responses provided by 24 L1 queer Armenian speakers between the ages of 16 and 44. Our data show that queer linguistic practices in Armenia are dominated by a marked preference for English over Armenian. Armenian is commonly avoided for the discussion of queer topics and identities because of a perceived lack of suitable queer vocabulary, negative associations between the Armenian language and past queerphobic trauma, the feeling that Armenian is not a safe linguistic space for queer people, and a view that Armenian culture and language is simply incompatible with queer identities. By contrast, English is widely regarded as a safe space for the community, with most new queer vocabulary acquired from international English-medium sources such as social media and TV. These findings provide a noteworthy case study of a queer community in which the national L1 plays a relatively small role and in which English, an international language, is perceived as the safest and most familiar linguistic vehicle for the expression of LGBTQIA+ identities and topics.

Key words: Armenia, queer language, belonging, communities of practice, South Caucasus

Queer Kinship as Method in Social Reproduction Research

Lima Boudakian and Cheaito

Abstract:

This paper argues that ‘queer kinship’ can be mobilized as a methodological lens in social reproduction research. Beginning from the everyday and working with a queer political economy lens, we argue that queer kinship economies can make visible the circuits of care otherwise erased in accounts of the household, both financial and non-financial. In theorizing this methodological lens, we draw on case studies of queer life in Yerevan (Armenia) and Beirut (Lebanon), and examine how LGBTQ+ individuals navigate structural exclusion from state welfare, labor markets, and familially-organised care. The paper examines queer ethnographic work and activist archives with labor market indicators, legal frameworks, NGO reports, and remittance data from both locations. In Armenia and Lebanon, diasporic and other legible financial flows are central to economic survival, yet often disinvest from queer lives and fractured kin ties while sustaining heteronormative family forms. This double movement—subsidising normative households while abandoning queer subjects—precipitates the growth of alternative, informal economies grounded in queer kinship, mutual care and survival labour. This paper argues that these growing alternative economies, including practices such as shared housing, collective caregiving, subsistence entrepreneurship, informal gig work and diasporic peer-to-peer support are not only strategies of endurance but also dissident forms of social reproduction. Attending to queer kinship economies thus offers a methodological and epistemological intervention into social reproduction research, revealing how capitalism’s crises are lived, contested and re-made through queer everyday life.

Tap Glass/Touch Grass: Social Media and the Lived Experience of Queer Youth in Armenia

Lima Boudakian

Abstract:

In what ways does the facilitation of identity discovery through social media communities impact the lived experience of queer youth in Armenia?

The anonymity and access to information provided by an otherwise-harmful social media world allows for queer people in Yerevan, Armenia to safely explore their identities and connect with each other. These connections, which are facilitated more safely through online platforms, have led to a variety of in-person spaces, including a queer-feminist library and drag shows. The paper uses local survey results (n=67) as a basis to analyze the role of social media in in-person community formation. Outside of these spaces, subtle signaling through clothing and makeup, which deviate from the rigidity of post-USSR Armenian dress, are slowly becoming more common in Yerevan. Armenia is a community-centered society, and these physical spaces are places where queer people can safely express affection and build community away from compulsory heterosexuality. Compulsory heterosexuality and pro-natalism are particularly prevalent in Armenia due to historical and recent instances of genocide, ethnic cleansing, and demographic crisis. Cultural rigidity has made the formation of visible subcultures difficult, in part due to a pre-social media lack of oppositional information. Access to English and Russian-language content has allowed for the expansion of information availability and subsequent facilitation of identity discovery. A strong regional precedent of social media being used for political movement-building has allowed for queer people to do the same without drawing extensive unwanted attention to themselves. However, the lack of language in Armenian related to gender and sexuality strengthen anti-queer stigma and reinforce pro-natalist and compulsory heterosexual attitudes, making broader cultural shifts difficult.
